Project 01

Tensile testing and FEBio modelling of silicone elastomers

Repeat tensile tests on three silicone geometries, a search for what the set-up was doing to the data, and a finite element model of the strip specimen.

  • Tinius Olsen UTM
  • Horizon software
  • Repeat-test protocol
  • HandySCAN 3D
  • FEBio Studio
Where
Swansea University
Context
EG-M321
Role
Individual
Year
2025

The question. How much does specimen geometry change the measured tensile response of a soft silicone, how repeatable are the measurements, and can a simple finite element model reproduce what the machine measures?

Protocol

VariableSetting
SpecimensThree geometries from the same silicone: standard dog-bone, irregular dog-bone, rectangular strip (45 × 20 × 2.5 mm)
Control modeDisplacement control, 40 mm/min crosshead speed
SamplingForce and position logged about every 0.2 s
RepeatsTwo samples per geometry, each tested twice
ChecksDimensions measured and entered in Horizon; visual inspection for defects; alignment with the loading axis
Data reductionForce–position to engineering stress–strain; modulus from the low-strain linear region

What the repeats showed

Reading the data criticallyI separated what the material was doing from what the set-up was doing. Low-strain fluctuations were traced to grip seating and alignment, and the small drop on reloading was attributed to stress softening rather than damage, because the slopes matched and no degradation appeared in later tests.

Geometry effects and the model comparison

When the plan did not work

I planned to build the finite element models from 3D scans of the real specimens. The HandySCAN captured the overall shapes, but the dog-bone meshes were too noisy and inconsistent to mesh reliably.

Rather than force a poor geometry into the model, I recorded the scan as a feasibility check, restricted the modelling to the rectangular strip, and defined it from measured nominal dimensions (Hex8 mesh, 30 × 6 × 3 elements, linear elastic, ν = 0.45). The decision and its reason are stated in the report.

Results

  • Good repeatability for all geometries, with differences confined mainly to the first few millimetres of grip engagement.
  • Specimen geometry changed the apparent stiffness and data quality, so geometry has to be fixed and reported if results are to be compared.
  • The simulated stress–strain response was linear (R² = 0.9999); the idealised clamp was adequate for estimating bulk stiffness at small strain.

What I would change next time

  • Test more specimens per geometry, so that specimen-to-specimen variation can be separated from test-to-test variation.
  • Measure strain on the specimen instead of from crosshead travel, which includes grip slip and machine compliance.
  • Use a hyperelastic or viscoelastic material model if behaviour beyond small strain matters.
